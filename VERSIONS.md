# hazeljs-examples: versions

Each version lists what the apps do as of that version. The newest version is first.

## 0.0.50 (2026-11-22)

- **Bricks: a paddle, a ball and a wall of bricks, its physics in Hazel** (ticket 824; Matthew: "let's do more classic games with simple physics and rules: break out, snake, tron"). Six levels, their bricks as Hazel data (rows, a pyramid, a checkerboard, a diamond between posts, a shelf of posts, a frame with a stair); a darker brick takes two hits, a grey post never breaks. Every step is Hazel's (Bricks.step): the paddle moved toward where you point, then the ball in four small steps off the walls, the paddle (its angle off it set by where it hits, up to 60 degrees) and the bricks; a ball lost, a level cleared (100 points). A finger or the mouse along the field moves the paddle, a tap sends the ball off; or ⬅️ ➡️ and the space bar; a wide paddle for a new player. 🤖 Hazel plays for you: Hazel's robot (Bricks.robot) works out where the ball will come down, off the walls, and sets the paddle to send it toward the column with the most bricks left; it cleared the first level without losing a ball. Compiled ahead of time by hazel-jit: a step in 0.004 ms; compiled and interpreted agree on 118 answers.

## 0.0.43 (2026-11-21)

- **Minesweeper: Hazel guesses as well as it can, and wins** (ticket 809, Matthew: "I'd like to revisit the minesweeper AI logic --- it seems quite likely for the largest board that it fails every time (or most times)"). It did: 🤖 won 2 expert games in 100. When no number alone shows a sure move, the page used to open a closed cell at random. Now Hazel picks (a new module, **Guess**): the numbers' closed cells fall into groups that share none; in each, every way the mines can lie that meets all its numbers is counted (up to a limit), by how many mines it uses; the groups put together, each way weighted by how many ways the mines left over can lie in the closed cells no number touches. A cell's odds of a mine are the weight of the ways with one there; the pick is the least likely cell (a corner, then an edge, when the untouched cells are as good). A group too big to count falls back to each cell's worst share, and the cells two numbers together show safe. A cell with no way to be a mine is sure: 🤖 opens it as a sure move, and 💡 shows it too ("the numbers around it, taken together, leave no way for a mine here"); else 💡 shows the best guess and its odds. Won over 200 games each: beginner 96% (was 82%), intermediate 89% (was 52%), expert 47% (was 2%). A pick takes 4 ms on average in the compiled rules, 77 ms at the slowest.

## 0.0.42 (2026-11-21)

- **Mahjong solitaire: Hazel's plan survives your own pairs** (ticket 807, the follow-up of ticket 803's measure: after twenty pairs taken at random, Hazel's play needed shuffles in most games). Hazel's plan is a pairing of the tiles, and an order to take them in. When you take two tiles that the plan had paired with others, their partners must pair with each other, and the plan has to change. 0.0.41 tried the new pair at four places in the old order; that hardly helped (with no shuffle, 29 of 60 games cleared after twenty random pairs, as before). Now the plan's pairs are played through as a set, not in their order: each turn, the first of them whose two tiles are both free. Taking tiles only ever frees others, so if any order of those pairs clears the table, this one does, and the order it finds is the new plan. When it gets stuck, the two pairs of one face are paired the other two ways, a face at a time, until one clears the table. After twenty random pairs, Hazel now clears 16 turtles, 17 pyramids and 9 fortresses of 20 with no shuffle (it was 9, 13 and 7); a solver finds at least 14, at least 16 and 10 of those positions winnable at all, so the shuffles left are mostly for games the random pairs had already lost. A take off the plan costs 8.5 ms on average in the compiled rules, 204 ms at the slowest (when no pairing works and every face is tried); a planned pair, 0.1 ms.

## 0.0.7 (2026-11-15)

Everything in 0.0.6, plus:

- **The Tarot deals about three and a half times faster.** Every function in its Hazel (`Deck.hz`, `Spreads.hz`, `Reading.hz`) now declares its whole type, what it returns as well as what it takes: `let take : ([Card], Int) -> (Card, [Card]) = fun (cs, k) -> ...`, where before only `(cs, k) : ([Card], Int)` was declared. Hazel leaves a result type that is not declared unknown, and checks a value against an unknown type as the program runs: at every return, and again on every walk down a list built that way (the deck, made by `cat`, `majors_from` and `suit_from`, was slow to walk for that reason). Declared, those checks go. A Celtic Cross took 173 ms in Node and takes 46 (70 in the browser); the year ahead, 296 and now 86. The deal is the same deal: every spread checked, no card twice.

## 0.0.2 (2026-11-15)

Everything in 0.0.1, plus the first public release, at https://matthewhammer.org/hazeljs-oracles/ :

- **Every spread on the list**, chosen from one menu, each with a line on what it is for: one card; three cards read three ways (past, present, future; situation, challenge, advice; mind, body, spirit); the Celtic Cross; a relationship (you, the other, the bond, its strength, its tension, a way forward); a choice between two or three paths (the crossroads, then each path's opportunity, difficulty and direction); the year ahead (a card for each month from this one, with or without a card for the year). The panel lists them newest first, with when and what came (the hexagram and the one it changes to, and how it was cast; the spread and its cards). Open one and it is shown as the reading just made, under its own heading, "Reading from" its date and time, with **New reading** beside it; ✕ forgets one, and **Forget them all** forgets every one, after asking.
- **The I Ching links to a fuller reading on another site** once six lines are cast (Cafe au Soul's, said plainly to be another site's, copyrighted and with ads; it gets only the six numbers).
- **The Celtic Cross's crossing card** lies across the first card without covering its neighbours, and a spread wider than its column scrolls.
